Operational risk

Operational risk is the risk of loss from inadequate or failed internal processes, people, systems, or external events — the broadest category in the Basel III taxonomy, covering process failure, fraud, legal risk, and conduct-related losses. Operational risk capital is held under the standardized approach or, for a bank that uses it, the Advanced Measurement Approach (AMA); operational-resilience requirements such as DORA add a resilience layer for ICT risk. The Risk and Control Self-Assessment (RCSA) and Key Risk Indicator (KRI) frameworks operationalize the risk appetite. The GenAI opportunity is to surface cross-system patterns — loss events, near-misses, KRI signals, and third-party performance — that manual monitoring misses between quarterly committee cycles.

Insights & analytics Operational risk events are logged across IT incident tracking, operational risk registers, service-desk tickets, and vendor notifications — each in a different system with a different taxonomy. The Head of Operational Risk synthesizes patterns from these sources manually each quarter; emerging cross-system clusters — a deployment-related defect cluster, a geographic ATM-fraud concentration — are typically identified three to six months after they emerged in the data.
Enablement RCSA facilitation — scoring inherent risk and control effectiveness across all business processes — is bottlenecked on specialist operational risk officers who guide each business unit through the assessment. With 30–50 business units each requiring an annual RCSA, the function is often under-resourced relative to the assessment schedule.
Automation The quarterly Operational Risk Committee pack — loss event summary, KRI dashboard, RCSA status, and emerging risk register — is assembled from multiple system exports and manually narrated each cycle. The inputs are structured and recurring; the narrative format is consistent.
New business opportunities An operational risk function with continuous event pattern detection shifts the Bank's posture from reactive loss reporting to forward-looking risk signal. Early identification of a failure pattern — before it generates a material loss event — enables control intervention at a fraction of the cost of post-event remediation.

Loss events & near-misses

Operational loss events are the realized losses from operational risk failures — recorded in the operational risk management system with event type, business line, amount, and root cause. Near-misses are events that could have produced a loss but did not — equally informative for risk management but recorded less systematically. Where a bank uses the Basel II Advanced Measurement Approach (AMA), internal loss data must meet minimum quality standards and cover at least five years of history. Loss data aggregated across the portfolio is the primary input to advanced capital modeling; thematic patterns in recent events inform RCSA updates.

Third-party & TPRM

Third-party risk management (TPRM) covers the governance and ongoing monitoring of risks arising from vendors, outsourcing partners, and ICT providers — including sub-outsourcing chains and intra-group dependencies. Operational-resilience requirements such as DORA call for a register of all ICT third-party providers with defined information requirements per vendor; outsourcing requirements set similar expectations. Critical vendor identification, contract due diligence, ongoing performance monitoring, and exit planning are the core TPRM disciplines. With 50–300 ICT providers, maintaining a current, accurate register is a significant operational undertaking.

RCSA & KRI monitoring

The Risk and Control Self-Assessment (RCSA) is the structured framework through which each business unit identifies its key operational risks, assesses inherent risk severity, and evaluates the effectiveness of controls in place. Key Risk Indicators (KRIs) are quantitative measures that signal changes in the operational risk environment — transaction error rates, system downtime, staff turnover, control failure rates — tracked against thresholds defined in the Risk Appetite Statement. Supervisory guidelines on operational risk expect documented RCSA processes and KRI frameworks. The RCSA cycle typically runs annually; KRI reporting runs monthly to the Operational Risk Committee.

Business continuity & DR

Business continuity management (BCM) and disaster recovery (DR) define the Bank's capacity to maintain critical business functions and restore IT systems following a disruption event. Under operational-resilience requirements, banks maintain tested BCM and DR plans, define Recovery Time Objectives (RTOs) and Recovery Point Objectives (RPOs) for critical systems, and test these against defined scenarios, typically at least annually. Post-incident review documentation — capturing root cause, recovery timeline, and lessons learned — is a regulatory expectation after any major disruption. BCP and DR plans require annual refresh to reflect changes in the operating environment.
