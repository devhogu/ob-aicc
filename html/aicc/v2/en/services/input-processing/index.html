Input processing

Every day a department receives a stream of documents and data: emails and requests, applications and questionnaires, scans and photos of documents, statements, registers, spreadsheets from partners, and requests from regulators. Usually an employee reads each document, types the information needed into a system, checks it against reference lists, and decides where to send it next. Input processing takes this work over: AI reads the incoming documents, breaks them down into fields, checks them, and passes them on in the right shape, while the employee deals with the exceptions.

What's included

  • Intake. Documents and data are collected from wherever they arrive: a mailbox, a shared folder, a form, an export.
  • Recognition. Text is recognized in any format, including scans, photos, spreadsheets, and PDFs, in the languages you need.
  • Breaking down into fields. The information needed is extracted from the document: parties, amounts, dates, bank details, numbers, the substance of a request.
  • Classification. The document is assigned a type, a topic, an urgency, and a recipient.
  • Checks. The information is checked for completeness and consistency: whether all fields are there, whether amounts and dates add up, and whether the details match the reference lists and what is already in the systems.
  • Standardizing. Different formats are turned into a uniform record or table, in the form the next step needs.
  • Passing on. The record goes to the right queue, system, or working file, and the original document stays next to it, so any figure can be checked against the source.
  • Exceptions. Anything that failed a check, or that AI isn't sure about, goes to an employee with a note on exactly what is wrong.

Every flow has an employee who is responsible for the result, and a clear rule on what AI does on its own and what a person must check. How deep the checks go depends on the risk tier: this is how the “human in the loop” approach works in practice.

What to bring

  • “Dozens of applications come in every day, and we type them into the system by hand.”
  • “Partners send registers in different formats, and we have to combine them by hand.”
  • “Requests come into a shared mailbox, and half the time goes into figuring out who to pass them to.”
  • “Before processing, every document has to be checked for completeness, and that takes longer than the processing itself.”
  • “Regulator requests come by email, and the answers have to be pulled together from several sources.”

How it differs from related services

Workspace automation helps employees cope with their own routine work. Input processing sits at the entrance to a process: it takes in everything that comes into the department from outside or from other departments and prepares it for work. The two services often work together: the incoming flow is sorted, and from there an assistant at the workplace supports the employee.

What you get

A working incoming flow: documents arrive, are broken down, and land where they are expected, and the employee sees only the exceptions. The flow has a description, a risk tier, check rules, a responsible person in the department, and metrics: how many documents went through without a person, how many went for review, and how long it took.

How the work goes

We start with one type of document: we gather examples, agree on which fields are needed and how to check them, and build the processing within one iteration as run-rate work. Then, on the real flow, we look at what share of documents goes through without a person and improve the rules. A whole incoming flow with several document types and systems becomes an initiative.

What other departments can reuse

Each document type we handle becomes a reusable package: a description of the fields, check rules, examples, and connections. The next department with similar documents doesn't have to start from scratch.
