FSB, Basel Committee and BIS on AI

← All regulators and actsInternational principlesReports of standard-setting bodiesreference point

Briefly

The Financial Stability Board (FSB), the Basel Committee on Banking Supervision (BCBS), and the Bank for International Settlements (BIS), together with its Financial Stability Institute (FSI), are international bodies whose standards national regulators carry over into their own rules. They have not yet issued a separate binding standard on AI: their position is set out in reports and statements. Alongside them works IOSCO, the international organization of securities regulators: in May 2026 it issued a toolkit for supervising AI.

What it means in practice

For the organization: the region's regulators are likely to build their AI requirements around the same topics as these reports: model risk, data quality, dependence on third-party providers, and cybersecurity. Preparing early costs less: know where AI is used in our work, who is responsible for each use, how models are checked, and which providers we depend on.

For an employee, the main takeaway is simple: AI usually does not create new kinds of risk but amplifies familiar ones. So the usual working rules apply to AI too:

  • Check an AI result the way you would check a colleague's work, especially if a decision about a customer or money depends on it. Generative AI can state made-up facts with confidence; this is called hallucination.
  • Do not treat an AI assistant as a person, and do not trust it more than it deserves.
  • Protect customer data: do not put it into tools that are not approved in your organization.
  • Be alert to fraud: generative AI makes it easier to fake voices, documents, and emails, so an unexpected request to transfer money or disclose data is worth double-checking through another channel.

Key ideas

  • Four vulnerabilities (FSB). The ones most likely to increase systemic risk are: dependence on third-party providers and their concentration; market correlations, when many firms use similar models and data; cyber risks; and model risk, data quality, and data governance. In addition, generative AI increases the risk of fraud and disinformation.
  • AI amplifies familiar risks (FSI). AI sharpens model risk and data privacy risks. New risks come mainly from generative AI: hallucination and anthropomorphism, when people start trusting a system as if it were a person.
  • Rules exist but are not sufficient everywhere. Most regulators have not issued separate AI rules for financial institutions, since existing rules already cover most of the risks. Areas that need more attention are governance, people's expertise and skills, model risk management, data governance, new market players, and third-party AI providers.
  • Digitalization of finance (BCBS). The Basel Committee looks at AI as one of a range of new technologies and providers and sets out eight implications for banks and supervisors.
  • AI and cybersecurity (BCBS). The most powerful AI models can help banks and supervisors find vulnerabilities, but in the hands of attackers they could materially change the speed and scale of cyber incidents.
  • Supervisory tools (IOSCO). A set of non-binding tools covers the whole lifecycle of an AI system: governance and risk management, third-party providers, disclosure, recordkeeping, and reporting.

Good to know

  • The FSB report on the financial stability implications of AI was published on 14 November 2024. It calls on regulators to monitor the use of AI more closely, check whether their rules are adequate, and take up AI tools themselves.
  • The Basel Committee's report "Digitalisation of finance" was published on 16 May 2024. On 20 May 2026, the Committee separately noted what new AI models mean for banks' cybersecurity, and it continues to monitor developments.
  • The FSI review of AI regulation in the financial sector (FSI Insights No. 63) was published on 12 December 2024. It reflects the authors' views, not the official position of the BIS.
  • IOSCO's final report on its AI supervisory toolkit came out in May 2026; before that, in March 2025, IOSCO published a consultation report on the use of AI in capital markets.
  • The reports themselves bind no one: requirements become mandatory when a national regulator introduces them.
  • All materials are published in English; we did not find official Russian translations.
