General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR)

← All regulators and actsEuropean UnionEU regulationreference point

Briefly

The GDPR is the European Union regulation on personal data protection (Regulation (EU) 2016/679), adopted on 27 April 2016 and applied since 25 May 2018. It is binding on everyone who processes personal data in the EU, and on companies outside the EU if they offer goods and services to people in the EU or monitor their behavior there. It is not binding on us, but it serves as the benchmark: the personal data laws of our region rest on the same concepts.

What it means in practice

When you read the terms of an AI service, a contract with a supplier, or its description of data protection, you will almost always find references to the GDPR: who is responsible for the data, who only processes it on someone else's behalf, where it is stored, and how it is deleted. Knowing these concepts helps you ask the supplier the right questions. But GDPR compliance does not replace the requirements of our own laws: local rules apply to us.

For an employee, the GDPR's main lesson is simple: a customer's personal data is used only for a clear purpose and only to the extent that purpose needs. If you want to put customer data into an AI tool, first check whether that tool is approved for such data, and don't share more than the task needs. More on this in the guide What you can and cannot share with AI.

Key ideas

  • Personal data. Any information about a person that can identify them directly or indirectly: a name, an account number, an address, a voice, a photo.
  • Lawful basis. Data may be processed only on one of the grounds the law sets out: for example, the person's consent, the performance of a contract with them, or a legal obligation.
  • Purpose and data minimization. Data is collected for a specific purpose, no more is taken than needed, and it is kept no longer than needed.
  • People's rights. A person can find out what data is held about them, correct it, ask for it to be deleted, and object to its processing.
  • Automated decisions. A person has the right not to be subject to a decision made by a machine alone, with no human involved, if it significantly affects them, for example whether they get a loan. This is where explainability and human in the loop matter.
  • Assessing the risks to people. Before risky processing of data, a data protection impact assessment is carried out. In essence, it is an impact assessment for personal data.

Good to know

  • Adopted on 27 April 2016, in force since 24 May 2016, and applied since 25 May 2018. It applies across the whole European Economic Area.
  • Oversight: the national data protection authorities of the EU countries; the European Data Protection Board (EDPB) ensures consistent application.
  • Fines for serious violations go up to €20 million or up to 4% of a company's worldwide annual turnover.
  • On AI: on 18 December 2024 the EDPB issued Opinion 28/2024 on personal data in AI models: when a model can be considered anonymous, and when “legitimate interest” can serve as the basis for developing it.
  • Possible changes: on 19 November 2025 the European Commission proposed amendments to the GDPR in the “Digital Omnibus” package, including on processing data to develop AI. As of October 2026 this is only a proposal: it is under discussion in the European Parliament and has not been adopted.
