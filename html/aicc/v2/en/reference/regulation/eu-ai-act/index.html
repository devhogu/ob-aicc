EU Artificial Intelligence Act (AI Act)

← All regulators and actsEuropean UnionEU regulationreference point

Briefly

The AI Act is a European Union regulation (Regulation (EU) 2024/1689) adopted by the European Parliament and the Council of the EU on 13 June 2024. It is binding in every EU country and applies to anyone who places AI systems on the EU market or uses them there. It is not binding on us: it is a benchmark that our suppliers work to and that regulators in other countries look at.

What it means in practice

Large suppliers of AI models and services operate in the European market, so they build their products to meet this law: they describe their models, label AI-generated content, and publish usage rules. When you choose or review a service, it helps to ask the supplier how it meets the AI Act. It is a quick way to judge how mature its approach is.

The law's main idea is simple: the more harm a system can do to people, the stricter the requirements. Our risk tiers work the same way. An assistant that drafts a letter from public information is one thing; a system that influences a credit decision about a person is something else entirely. If you have an idea for an AI solution that affects customers, first work out its risk tier together with the team.

Key ideas

  • Four levels of risk. The law sorts AI systems into four levels: unacceptable risk (banned), high risk (strict requirements), limited risk (transparency duties), and minimal risk (almost no requirements). This is the risk-based approach in its purest form.
  • Prohibited practices. Some uses of AI are banned outright, for example harmful AI-based manipulation and deception, social scoring of citizens, and emotion recognition at work and in education.
  • High-risk AI. Systems that affect people's lives and rights. Among its examples the European Commission names credit scoring that decides whether a person gets a loan, and CV sorting in recruitment. Such systems need risk assessment and mitigation, high-quality data, activity logs, documentation, human oversight, robustness, and security.
  • Transparency. People must know when they are dealing with a machine, such as a chatbot, and AI-generated content must be identifiable.
  • General-purpose models. Separate rules apply to providers of the large models that many services are built on. A voluntary code of practice helps them meet these rules.
  • AI literacy. Organizations that build or use AI must help their staff understand it.

Good to know

  • It entered into force on 1 August 2024 and applies in stages: the bans from February 2025, the rules for general-purpose models from August 2025, and most of the law, including the transparency rules, from 2 August 2026.
  • In 2026 the EU adopted the “Digital Omnibus on AI” amendments (Regulation (EU) 2026/1744 of 8 July 2026). They moved the high-risk requirements: to 2 December 2027 for uses such as credit scoring, and to 2 August 2028 for AI built into products that the EU already regulates.
  • The same amendments added new bans (on generating intimate images without consent and child sexual abuse material) from 2 December 2026, and softened the literacy rule: organizations must now support the development of AI literacy among their staff.
  • Oversight: national authorities in the EU countries; general-purpose models are supervised by the European AI Office within the European Commission.
  • Fines for prohibited practices go up to €35 million or up to 7% of a company's worldwide annual turnover, whichever is higher.
