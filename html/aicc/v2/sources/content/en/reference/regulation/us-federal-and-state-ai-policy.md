## Briefly {#short}

The United States has no single federal AI law. Federal policy is set by presidential executive orders and rules for government agencies, and it changes with each administration: in 2025 the new administration revoked the 2023 order on safe AI and made US leadership in AI, with a minimum of restrictions, its main goal. Meanwhile, individual states pass AI laws that are binding within their borders and protect consumers. None of this is binding on us; it is a guide to where the market our suppliers work in is heading.

## What it means in practice {#practice}

Most of the AI models and services used around the world are built by American companies. So US policy shapes their usage rules, their transparency requirements, and even which services are available in which countries. When a supplier changes its terms or features, this is often the reason.

State laws are worth knowing for a different reason: they show which requirements for AI in consumer services are becoming the norm: tell people when an automated system makes a decision about them, explain a refusal in plain words, and offer a review by a human. This matches how we approach AI solutions that affect customers: [[explainability]] and [[human-in-the-loop|human in the loop]].

## Key ideas {#ideas}

- **Executive orders instead of a law.** Federal AI policy rests on presidential executive orders. A new president can revoke them, so the course changes with the administration.
- **Leadership with minimal restrictions.** Current federal policy aims for US global leadership in AI and the removal of barriers to its development.
- **Rules for the government.** Some requirements cover how federal agencies themselves use and buy AI. For example, uses of AI that may affect people's rights or safety are marked as “high-impact AI” and call for extra care, and in purchasing, agencies favor American AI systems and services.
- **Uniform rules versus state laws.** The administration believes that differing state laws hold back AI, and it is pushing for a single federal approach that would override state laws that conflict with it.
- **State consumer protection laws.** States regulate automated decisions about people, for example in hiring, housing, lending, and insurance: notice, explanation, and the right to a review.

## Good to know {#details}

- On 23 January 2025 the executive order “Removing Barriers to American Leadership in Artificial Intelligence” revoked Executive Order 14110 of 30 October 2023 on safe, secure, and trustworthy AI.
- In April 2025 the Office of Management and Budget (OMB) issued two memos: on the use of AI by federal agencies (M-25-21) and on the government's purchasing of AI (M-25-22).
- On 11 December 2025 Executive Order 14365, “Ensuring a National Policy Framework for Artificial Intelligence,” directed the Department of Justice to set up a task force to challenge state AI laws in court, the Department of Commerce to evaluate state laws, and the president's advisers to prepare a federal bill. The order proposes leaving alone state laws on child safety, on data centers, and on the states' own purchasing of AI.
- On 20 March 2026 the White House published recommendations to Congress on national AI rules, including replacing state laws with federal ones. These are only proposals: they become binding if Congress passes a law. Until then, state laws remain in force.
- An example state is Colorado. Its 2024 consumer protection law on AI has been replaced: on 14 May 2026 the governor signed SB 26-189 on automated decision-making technology. It applies from 1 January 2027 and covers, among other areas, financial and lending services: the person must be notified, must receive, within 30 days after an adverse decision, a plain-language description of the role the system played in it, and must be offered a review by a human.
