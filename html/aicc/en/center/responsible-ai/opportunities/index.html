The opportunities

AI todayOpportunitiesFintechRisksResponsible AIThe Bank's rulesTerms

Every wave of technology promises more than it delivers at first and more than anyone expected in the end. Generative AI is in its early years. Its value is real but uneven, and organizations earn it through the unglamorous work of governance, data, and adoption. This page describes where that value is appearing in banking, what the evidence says, and where the Bank has chosen to look first.

1. What changed

¶1.1. Three capabilities became cheap at once. Language: a machine that reads, writes, summarizes, and translates at a useful level, in any language the Bank works in. Knowledge: the ability to answer from a body of documents, with citations, as questions arise. Code: the ability to draft, explain, test, and repair software. Each existed before in narrow forms. What is new is that they are general, available through an interface anyone can use, and improving every quarter.

¶1.2. The economics of the opportunity are also new. Earlier automation replaced whole tasks and needed long change efforts. Generative AI helps with tasks that were never worth automating: the thousand small acts of reading, drafting, checking, and consolidating that fill a working day. It does so without a large build, through an assistant at the desk. The gains amount to minutes per task across many people. They materialize only where people are trained, the knowledge is governed, and the Solutions are approved.

2. Where the value appears in a bank

Where What generative AI does What it changes
Customer service and channels Real-time knowledge support for customer service representatives, interaction summaries, drafted responses, triage and routing of requests, detection of repeat contacts Shorter handling times, consistent answers, fewer repeat contacts, customer service representatives freed for difficult cases

¶2.1. The pattern across the table is the same: the machine drafts, assembles, or proposes; a person decides, approves, or signs. Where the industry has tried to remove the person from the loop in regulated work, results have been poor and regulators have objected. Where it has kept the person and removed the drudgery, results have held.

3. What the evidence says

¶3.1. Published studies from the first years agree on a few points. Productivity gains are measurable and largest for routine language and code work and for less experienced staff, who move closer to the level of experienced staff. Task-level gains do not become function-level gains without changes to processes, roles, and measures. Most pilots that fail do so for reasons that are not technical: unclear ownership, no measure of success, data that was not ready, people who were not trained, or a use that should never have left the sandbox. The cost of the technology falls quickly while the cost of organizational work does not. That is why organizations that invested early in governance, knowledge, and skills pull ahead.

¶3.2. For a bank, the implication is plain. The value lies more in small, governed uses across every function, on a shared platform, with trained people, than in a few large bets. That is the shape of the Bank's Strategic Priorities.

4. Where the Bank looks first

¶4.1. The Statement of Intent names the top Strategic Priorities of the Bank for AI, in order: customer intelligence, business intelligence, adoption within Domains, expertise at the point of work, AI in banking operations and systems, information technology operations and service lifecycle, and software engineering. Each rests on a governed foundation of people, data, and a shared AI Platform. Each advances through the Maturity Levels at its own pace.

¶4.2. The Statement of Intent gives first priority to customer intelligence and business intelligence (Statement of Intent 2.2). Within the priorities, the first steps are those with the best ratio of value to risk: approved assistants for employees on governed knowledge, knowledge bases for functions with the largest corpora, and automation of routine work reviewed by a person. AI agents, and AI that decides without review, follow at the higher Risk Tiers and the later Maturity Levels as the governance and the platform mature. The page AI in fintech and digital banking places this in the context of the Bank's own model. The risks and challenges explains why the order is not caution for its own sake.
