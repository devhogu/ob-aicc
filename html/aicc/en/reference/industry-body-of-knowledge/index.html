Standards and frameworks

OverviewStandards and frameworksRegulators and actsResearch and insightLearning and open resources

The charter of AICC is its own, and it draws on the common body of knowledge of the field: the frameworks for managing the risk of AI, the standards for managing it as a system, the principles that governments have agreed, and the practice of model risk, service management, and agile and lean delivery. This page lists the sources that matter to the Bank, in one line each, with where the charter uses them.

This is the first edition, a curated list for orientation, kept by the AICC Lead. Each source has a page under Reference; the laws and the supervisors are on the page Regulators and acts.

How to take them: a principle says why and binds no one; a standard is a yardstick an auditor or a partner may hold the Bank to, certifiable or not; a framework is a method to borrow; a practice is what the field does. The charter adopts them in its own terms and names none of their vocabulary, so that the Bank's rule stays readable on its own and can be mapped to any of them when asked.

1. The principles governments agreed

Source Issued by What it is Where the charter uses it
OECD Principles on Artificial Intelligence Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development The first intergovernmental standard for trustworthy AI, with the reference definition of an AI system The Principles of Application (Statement of Intent 6)
UNESCO Recommendation on the Ethics of Artificial Intelligence UNESCO, adopted by the member states including the Kyrgyz Republic The global normative instrument on the ethics of AI The values and the Principles of Application (Statement of Intent 4 and 6)
Council of Europe Framework Convention on Artificial Intelligence Council of Europe The first international treaty on AI, on human rights, democracy, and the rule of law Contestability and human oversight (Statement of Intent 6.6; AI Policy 3)
G7 Hiroshima AI Process The Group of Seven Voluntary commitments of the developers of advanced AI The provider assessment (AI Policy 4)

2. The standards and frameworks for AI

Source Issued by What it is Where the charter uses it
AI Risk Management Framework (AI RMF 1.0), with its Generative AI Profile National Institute of Standards and Technology, United States A voluntary framework to govern, map, measure, and manage the risks of AI systems across their life cycle The Risk Tiers and the gates before use (AI Policy 3); the AI Incident review (AI Policy 5); the measures of the Maturity Levels
ISO/IEC 42001, Artificial intelligence management system International Organization for Standardization and International Electrotechnical Commission Requirements for establishing, implementing, maintaining, and improving an AI management system in an organization The control loops and the controls (Operating Model 6 and 8); the AI Policy; the records and evidence (Operating Model 7)
ISO/IEC 23894, Guidance on AI risk management ISO and IEC Guidance on integrating risk management into AI activities, built on ISO 31000 The Risk Tiers (AI Policy 3); the AI Risk Appetite Statement (AICC Charter 5)
ISO/IEC 22989, AI concepts and terminology ISO and IEC The vocabulary of the field The Vocabulary of the charter
The risk-based approach of the EU Artificial Intelligence Act European Union Obligations graded by the risk of the use, from prohibited practices to high-risk systems with conformity, oversight, and transparency duties The reasoning behind the Risk Tiers (AI Policy 3), as a reference and not as an obligation of the Bank
OWASP Top 10 for Large Language Model Applications Open Worldwide Application Security Project The ten security risks of applications built on language models: prompt injection, sensitive information disclosure, supply chain, data and model poisoning, improper output handling, excessive agency, system prompt leakage, vector and embedding weaknesses, misinformation, unbounded consumption The security test against attacks on AI in the validation of Risk Tier 2 and 3 (AI Policy 3.3); the guardrails of a Solution

3. The practice of the financial sector

Source Issued by What it is Where the charter uses it
Supervisory guidance on model risk management (SR 11-7) Board of Governors of the Federal Reserve System and Office of the Comptroller of the Currency, United States The reference practice for the independent validation, documentation, and monitoring of models in banks The independence of the check and the validation (AI Policy 3; Statement of Intent 7.3); the Control Sign-Off
Reports on artificial intelligence in the financial sector Financial Stability Board; Basel Committee on Banking Supervision; Bank for International Settlements The view of the standard-setters on the benefits, the risks, and the supervisory expectations of AI in finance The AI Risk Appetite Statement (AICC Charter 5); the reporting to the Board Committee (AICC Charter 7)

4. The practice of management and delivery

Source What it is Where the charter uses it
The common body of agile and lean practice, and its codification for delivery at scale in the Scaled Agile Framework Delivery in fixed increments and iterations with planning, demonstration, and improvement at each level; work in levels worded as hypotheses with acceptance criteria; Kanbans with limits on work in progress; a program board of dependencies; a pipeline of exploration, integration, deployment, and release on demand; built-in quality; flow measured rather than effort The Solution Lifecycle Model, in the Bank's own terms: the levels (3), the boards (4), the cadence and its loops (6), verification and release (7), the measures (10)
Lean portfolio management practice, codified in the Lean Portfolio Management competency of the Scaled Agile Framework Strategic themes, investment envelopes and guardrails, a portfolio Kanban with limits on work in progress, a lean business case with a minimum viable product, weighted shortest job first, continuous governance The Portfolio Management Model, in the Bank's own terms
IT service management practice Requests, incidents, changes, and service levels handled through a service desk The support of a Service (Business Model 4.2); Service Management (Records and systems)
Internal control and assurance practice Control objectives, owners, evidence, and independent assurance The controls and the control catalogue (Operating Model 8); the Unit governance guide
