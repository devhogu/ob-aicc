The financial standard-setters on AI: FSB, BCBS, BIS

The bodies that set the standards national banking supervisors implement have published their views on AI in finance: its benefits, its risks to institutions and to the system, and what supervisors should expect. Their reports are the best indication of what the Bank's supervisor will ask in time.

1. Identity

Item Entry
Kind Reports and principles from financial standard-setters
Issued or overseen by Financial Stability Board; Basel Committee on Banking Supervision; Bank for International Settlements and its Innovation Hub
Jurisdiction Global
Status Reports on AI in finance and on the digitalization of finance, 2017 onward and renewed in 2024; implemented through national supervisors

2. What it sets

¶2.1. Recurring themes include model risk and model governance; third-party and concentration risk among AI providers; data quality and privacy; operational resilience and cyber risk; explainability and consumer protection; the risk of herding and correlated behavior across institutions; and the need for supervisors to build their own capability.

3. Whom it reaches

¶3.1. The standard-setters bind no bank directly; national supervisors translate their work into regulation and supervisory practice.

4. Relevance to the Bank

¶4.1. The expectations of the National Bank of the Kyrgyz Republic and regional supervisors will follow these themes. Early alignment with them is cheaper than catching up.

5. How the charter relates to it

¶5.1. The AI Risk Appetite Statement of the AI Competence Center Charter, the provider rules of the AI Policy, the quarterly risk check, and the escalations and reporting that the Executive Sponsor brings to the Board Committee address the themes identified by the standard-setters.

Read further

  • Reference

    OECD Principles on Artificial Intelligence

    The first intergovernmental standard on AI, adopted by the OECD members and adhering countries and taken up by the G20. It states what trustworthy AI is and what governments should do to foster it, and it supplies the definition of an AI system that most later instruments adopted.

  • Reference

    UNESCO Recommendation on the Ethics of Artificial Intelligence

    The global normative instrument on the ethics of AI, adopted by the member states of UNESCO. It frames AI in terms of human rights, human dignity, the environment, diversity, and peaceful societies, and it asks states to act through policy, assessment, and education.

  • Reference

    Council of Europe Framework Convention on Artificial Intelligence

    The first international treaty on AI. It commits the states that ratify it to ensure that the activities within the life cycle of AI systems are consistent with human rights, democracy, and the rule of law, in the public sector and, as each state decides, in the private sector.

  • Reference

    G7 Hiroshima AI Process

    The Group of Seven initiative on advanced AI produced guiding principles and a voluntary code of conduct for organizations developing the most capable models. These cover safety testing, transparency, incident reporting, and the responsible release of those models.

  • Reference

    ISO/IEC standards on AI: 42001, 23894, 22989

    These international standards give AI governance a form familiar to organizations from quality, security, and service management: a management system with policy, roles, risk assessment, controls, monitoring, and improvement, together with guidance on AI risk management and the vocabulary of the field.

  • Reference

    OWASP Top 10 for Large Language Model Applications

    The security community's list of the ten most important risks of applications built on large language models, maintained by the project that gave the web its best-known security list. It is the reference for the security test of an AI Solution.
