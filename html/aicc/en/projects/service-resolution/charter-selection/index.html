Customer Intelligence–Enabled Service Resolution — Candidate Project Charters

Customer Intelligence–Enabled Service Resolution offers three selectable project charters. One customer journey is selected, its ownership and baseline fields are completed, its proposed targets are confirmed, and it enters the bank's normal project-initiation process.

The Technical Pilot Blueprint defines the common banking-systems, customer-intelligence, GenAI, and agentic solution used to implement the selected charter.

The journey technical profiles define the systems, context, knowledge, AI-assistant functions, tools, control boundaries, and validation expected for each candidate. The platform capability and readiness map defines what IT must assess and provide without assuming a particular bank product or runtime.

Charter approval authorizes mobilization, baseline work, control definition, and pilot preparation. Controlled live use remains a separate readiness decision, and pilot approval does not authorize broader rollout.

Candidate charter Best fit Primary customer outcome Relative complexity
Payment Issue Resolution high payment-contact volume and fragmented payment status fewer repeat contacts and faster confirmed resolution lower
Card Dispute Progress Support repeated status calls and evidence confusion during disputes clearer progress and fewer avoidable status contacts medium
Onboarding/KYC Progress Support stalled applications and repeated information requests faster progression with one clear request for valid information medium–high

Selection rule

Select the journey with:

  • material repeat-contact or stalled-case volume;
  • one accountable process owner;
  • a stable existing resolution process;
  • accessible case and contact records;
  • a measurable customer outcome;
  • a service team able to operate the controlled pilot.

Common acceptance logic

Each candidate charter uses five measure groups:

  1. Customer outcome: the journey-specific problem improves against baseline.
  2. Operational benefit: investigation, handling, handoff, or rework improves.
  3. Adoption and acceptance: employees use the capability and judge it useful.
  4. Quality: facts, statuses, blockers, and actions are supported and sufficiently accurate.
  5. Customer protection and control limits: complaints, customer treatment, privacy, and control outcomes remain within agreed limits.

Proposed targets are starting thresholds. The Sponsor, Journey Process Owner, Service Quality Lead, and relevant control functions confirm or replace them after the baseline is established and before controlled live use.

The project passes only when all mandatory quality and control-limit criteria pass and the primary outcome provides sufficient evidence for the Sponsor's stated scale decision.

Minimum evidence expectation

The charter sizes evidence according to the question being answered:

Evidence purpose Realistic planning expectation Why it is needed
Historical quality and failure analysis normally 200–500 manually reviewed cases for one defined journey scope, with deliberate coverage of every common state, blocker, and material exception demonstrates that the timeline, status, blocker, and proposed action are correct across the journey; raw volume cannot replace state and exception coverage
Live workflow and employee acceptance normally at least 100 eligible cases across a representative group of service employees exposes workflow friction, correction behavior, training needs, adoption, and operational failure modes
Customer-outcome evidence calculated from the baseline outcome rate, target improvement, eligible volume, and required confidence; high-volume journeys may require roughly 1,000–3,000 cases in each pilot/comparison population repeat contact and progression are binary or time-based outcomes; a small usability sample cannot reliably demonstrate a modest business improvement

For illustration, reducing a 30% repeat-contact rate by 15% relatively means moving it to 25.5%, an absolute change of 4.5 percentage points. Demonstrating that difference reliably commonly requires well over 1,000 eligible cases in each comparison population. The Measurement Owner calculates the actual requirement after baseline. Lower-volume journeys may use a longer observation period, a larger relative target, or directional outcome evidence with an explicitly limited scale decision.

The charter records:

  • eligibility and exclusion rules;
  • metric formula and denominator;
  • baseline and comparison design;
  • outcome observation period;
  • required coverage of process states and material exceptions;
  • minimum live-workflow sample;
  • customer-outcome sample or directional-evidence rationale;
  • treatment of reopened, transferred, abandoned, and incomplete cases.

These ranges are planning expectations rather than quotas. Common categories need enough reviewed examples to reveal recurring performance, while rare high-severity exceptions may require review of every available case. Coverage and the decision to be supported take precedence over an arbitrary total.

Control-limit expectation

Before live use, every charter names each control-limit metric, source, owner, tolerance, review frequency, and suspension threshold. Zero-tolerance events are stated separately from monitored non-inferiority measures.

Typical zero-tolerance events include missed legal or scheme deadlines caused by the capability, loss of customer rights, unauthorized disclosure, an unreviewed customer communication, bypass of a mandatory control, and critical customer harm. Complaints, corrections, customer satisfaction, unresolved-case age, accessibility, and vulnerable-customer outcomes use baseline-informed tolerances.
