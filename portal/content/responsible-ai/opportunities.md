# The opportunities

Every wave of technology promises more than it delivers at first and more than anyone expected in the end. Generative AI is in its first years, and the honest view is that its value is real, uneven, and earned by the organizations that do the unglamorous work of governance, data, and adoption. This page states where the value is appearing in banking, what the evidence says, and where the Bank has chosen to look first.

## 1. What changed

1.1. Three capabilities became cheap at once. Language: a machine that reads, writes, summarizes, and translates at a useful level, in any language the Bank works in. Knowledge: the ability to answer from a body of documents, with citations, at the moment the question is asked. Code: the ability to draft, explain, test, and repair software. Each existed before in narrow forms; what is new is that they are general, available through an interface anyone can use, and improving every quarter.

1.2. The economic shape of the opportunity is also new. Earlier automation replaced whole tasks and needed long projects. Generative AI assists tasks that were never worth automating, the thousand small acts of reading, drafting, checking, and consolidating that fill a working day, and it does so without a project, through a tool at the desk. The gains come in minutes per task across many people, and they are realized only where people are trained, the knowledge is governed, and the tools are approved.

## 2. Where the value appears in a bank

| Where | What generative AI does | What it changes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Customer service and channels | Real-time knowledge assist for the agent, summaries of the interaction, drafted responses, triage and routing of requests, detection of repeat contacts | Shorter handling, consistent answers, fewer repeat contacts, agents freed for the difficult cases |
| Operations and servicing | Extraction from documents and forms, consolidation of inputs, drafting of standard documents, reconciliation break triage, case narratives | Shorter cycle times and fewer errors in the routine, with decisions still taken by qualified persons |
| Compliance and financial crime | Investigation packs assembled, narratives drafted, false positives triaged, regulatory change digested | Analysts spend their time on judgment rather than assembly; consistent documentation |
| Finance, treasury, and reporting | Report editions drafted from governed data, variance commentary, question answering over the figures, consistency checks across submissions | Faster closes and editions, traceable figures, fewer manual steps |
| Risk and audit | Risk synthesis, dossier assembly, validation report drafting, audit work-paper pre-population, evidence quality checks | More coverage with the same people; the human effort goes to the assessment |
| Knowledge and expertise | Policies, procedures, product rules, and regulations as governed knowledge, answered with citations at the point of work | Consistent and compliant service, faster proficiency of new staff, institutional knowledge retained |
| Technology and operations | Coding assistance, code review, test generation, legacy code explanation, incident triage, runbook execution under approval | Faster and safer delivery; more stable operations |
| Strategy and management | Steering pre-reads, scenario analysis, Board narratives drafted from governed sources, benefit tracking | Better-founded decisions and consistent communication with investors |
| The digital bank as a whole | Onboarding and identity checks, real-time fraud scoring, chat-first service, thin-file credit, operations behind the application | The fintech model at scale, with the risks the next page sets out |

2.1. The pattern across the table is the same: the machine drafts, assembles, or proposes; a person decides, approves, or signs. Where the industry has tried to remove the person from the loop in regulated work, the results have been poor and the regulators have objected. Where it has kept the person and removed the drudgery, the results have held.

## 3. What the evidence says

3.1. The published studies of the first years agree on a few points. Productivity gains are measurable and are largest for routine language and code work and for less experienced staff, who are lifted toward the level of the experienced. Gains at the level of a task do not become gains at the level of a function without changes to the process, the roles, and the measures. Most pilots that fail do so for reasons that are not technical: unclear ownership, no measure of success, data that was not ready, people who were not trained, or a use that should never have left the sandbox. And the cost of the technology falls quickly while the cost of the organizational work does not, which is why the organizations that invested early in governance, knowledge, and skills pull ahead.

3.2. For a bank the implication is plain. The value is in the breadth of small, governed uses across every function, on a shared platform, with trained people, more than in a few large bets. That is the shape of the Bank's Strategic Priorities.

## 4. Where the Bank looks first

4.1. The Statement of Intent names seven Strategic Priorities, in order: customer intelligence, business intelligence, adoption within Domains, expertise at the point of work, AI in banking operations and systems, information technology operations, and software engineering. Each rests on a governed foundation of people, data, and a shared AI Platform, and each advances through the Maturity Levels at its own pace.

4.2. The first steps are the ones with the best ratio of value to risk: approved assistants for employees on governed knowledge, knowledge bases for the functions with the largest corpora, and the automation of routine work under human validation. Embedded AI in operations, intelligence over customer data, and agents follow as the governance and the platform mature. The page AI in fintech and digital banking sets this in the context of the Bank's own model, and The risks and challenges explains why the order is not caution for its own sake.
