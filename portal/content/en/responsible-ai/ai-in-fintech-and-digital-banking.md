# AI in fintech and digital banking

Digital banks and fintech firms were the first in finance to use AI at scale, because their model made it natural: they are data-rich, built on software and interfaces rather than branches, and they grow by handling volume with few people. This page sets the Bank's use of AI in that context: what the fintech landscape does with AI, what it means for a mobile-first bank in this region, what the financial supervisors watch, and what the Bank takes from it.

## 1. The landscape

1.1. The fintech landscape has four kinds of player. Payment firms and wallets, which move money and know every transaction. Neobanks and digital banks, which offer accounts, cards, and credit through an application and run their operations as software. Specialized lenders, which score and serve customers that traditional credit files miss. And the incumbents that digitalized, which combine the data of a bank with the methods of a fintech. The super-apps of several regions, which bundle payments, commerce, and services, are the extreme form of the first two.

1.2. All of them share the features that make AI natural: every interaction leaves data; every process is already a system that software can read and act in; customers are served through channels where an assistant can sit beside the customer service representative or in front of the customer; and the economics reward handling more with the same people. The same features raise the stakes: errors scale as fast as service does, fraud follows the money, and the customers who are easiest to reach digitally are often the ones the law protects most.

## 2. Where fintech uses AI, and the risk beside each

| Where | What AI does | The risk beside it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Onboarding and know-your-customer | Reads identity documents, checks liveness, matches faces and names, scores the risk of a new customer, routes the exceptions to a person | A wrong rejection excludes a person; a wrong acceptance lets a bad actor in; the data is the most sensitive the Bank holds |
| Fraud and transaction monitoring | Scores transactions in real time, finds patterns across accounts and devices, prioritizes alerts, drafts the investigation | False positives block honest customers; false negatives cost money; the models drift as the schemes change |
| Credit for thin-file customers | Scores from transaction, mobile, and behavioral data where no credit history exists; proposes limits; monitors repayment | Bias against groups the data under-represents; decisions that cannot be explained; the use the regulators treat as high risk |
| Personalization and offers | Ranks the next best offer, times the message, tailors the product | Manipulation and unfair treatment; pricing that discriminates; consent for the data used |
| Chat-first service and resolution | Answers questions from governed knowledge, drafts responses, detects repeat contacts, proposes the next step on a case | Confident wrong answers to customers; the customer who cannot reach a person; data in the conversation |
| Collections | Segments by likelihood and reason, drafts contact, proposes arrangements | Pressure on vulnerable customers; the duties of fair treatment |
| Regulatory technology | Screens against sanctions and politically exposed persons, assembles reports, monitors regulatory change, drafts narratives | A regulated process whose controls and audit trail must stay intact |
| Open banking and partner interfaces | Reads and reconciles partner data, monitors the interfaces, detects anomalies | Third-party and concentration risk; the data that crosses the boundary |
| Treasury and liquidity in a payments business | Forecasts flows, watches intraday positions, drafts commentary | Decisions on funds that remain with people |
| Operations behind the application | Reconciles, investigates payment exceptions, handles disputes and chargebacks, drafts standard documents | Errors at volume; the reversibility of what was done |

2.1. The pattern holds across the table: the machine scores, drafts, and proposes; a person decides where the law or the risk requires; and the controls of the process stay in place. Where a fintech removed the person from a regulated decision, the regulator or the market eventually put the person back.

## 3. What is particular here

3.1. A digital bank in Central Asia works in conditions that shape its use of AI. Customers are mobile-first and reach the bank through the application, the contact center, and bank representatives, so the assistant that helps staff and the knowledge that answers the customer matter more than in a branch bank. Two languages are in daily use with customers, Kyrgyz and Russian, so language work is a real cost and a real opportunity. A large part of the economy runs on cash and remittances, so onboarding, payments, and inclusion are where value and risk meet. Financial inclusion is a Strategic Pillar of the Bank, and the Statement of Intent ties AI to it: digital onboarding and identification, service in the languages of the country, and responsible lending decisions that extend access. And the supervisors of the region are forming their expectations of AI now, from the work of the global standard-setters and the practice of the neighboring markets, so a bank that governs early helps shape what is expected and is ready when it is.

## 4. What the financial supervisors and standard-setters watch

4.1. The supervisors of finance did not write new rulebooks for AI; they read AI through the rules they have. The expectations below recur across them, and each has a reference page in this site.

| What they watch | The expectation | The bodies and instruments |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Model risk | Models are inventoried, validated by people without a stake in them, documented, and monitored, in proportion to their materiality | [The United States supervisory guidance on model risk](../reference/regulations/us-model-risk-and-consumer-guidance.md); [the Basel Committee](../reference/regulations/financial-standard-setters-on-ai.md); [the National Bank of the Kyrgyz Republic](../reference/regulations/nbkr.md) and the regional supervisors |
| Fairness and explainability in credit | A credit decision does not discriminate and can be explained to the person it affects, whatever the model | [The EU AI Act](../reference/regulations/eu-ai-act.md), which treats credit scoring of natural persons as high risk; [the consumer regulator of the United States](../reference/regulations/us-model-risk-and-consumer-guidance.md); the consumer protection rules of [the National Bank of the Kyrgyz Republic](../reference/regulations/nbkr.md) |
| Operational resilience and third parties | Incidents are reported, resilience is tested, and the providers of technology and AI are assessed, contracted, monitored, and replaceable | [The EU Digital Operational Resilience Act and the European Banking Authority](../reference/regulations/eu-dora.md); [the Financial Stability Board](../reference/regulations/financial-standard-setters-on-ai.md) on third-party and concentration risk; the outsourcing rules of [the National Bank of the Kyrgyz Republic](../reference/regulations/nbkr.md) |
| Consumer protection in automated service | Customers know when they deal with AI, are treated fairly, can reach a person, and can contest a decision | [The Council of Europe convention](../reference/regulations/council-of-europe-ai-convention.md); [the EU AI Act](../reference/regulations/eu-ai-act.md) and [the GDPR](../reference/regulations/eu-gdpr.md); the consumer protection rules of the national supervisors |
| Data protection | Personal data is used lawfully, minimally, securely, within the rules of residency and transfer | The personal data laws of [the Kyrgyz Republic](../reference/regulations/kg-personal-information-law.md), [Kazakhstan](../reference/regulations/kz-personal-data-law.md), and [the Russian Federation](../reference/regulations/ru-personal-data-law.md); [the GDPR](../reference/regulations/eu-gdpr.md) as their model |
| Effectiveness against financial crime | Know-your-customer, monitoring, and reporting keep their controls and their audit trail when AI assists them | [The anti-money-laundering legislation and bodies](../reference/regulations/kg-aml-body.md); the standards of the Financial Action Task Force |
| The system as a whole | Herding, concentration on few providers, and the capability of supervisors themselves | [The Financial Stability Board, the Basel Committee, and the Bank for International Settlements](../reference/regulations/financial-standard-setters-on-ai.md) |

4.2. The [Regulators and acts](../reference/regulators-and-acts.md) page lists the bodies and the instruments by jurisdiction, with a page for each, and the [Acts and compliance](../knowledge-base/acts-and-compliance.md) page states what the applicable ones require of a use of AI at the Bank.

## 5. What the Bank takes from it

5.1. The Bank gives first priority to customer intelligence and business intelligence (Statement of Intent 2.2), and within them the first steps that fit a digital bank are the ones with the best ratio of value to risk: assistants for employees on governed knowledge; knowledge services for the functions with the largest corpora; the automation of operations behind the application, reviewed by a person; and assistance to fraud, onboarding, and servicing under the validation of the Control Functions. The steps that come later are the ones the supervisors watch most: credit decisions shaped by AI, customer-facing AI agents, and AI agents that act on funds, which the Bank places at the higher Risk Tiers and at the later Maturity Levels.

5.2. The Bank's own rules, the AI Policy, the Risk Tiers, and the gates before use, are built to let it move on the first steps now and to be ready for the later ones when the governance, the platform, and the supervisor are.
