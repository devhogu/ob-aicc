## Briefly {#short}

The Council of Europe Framework Convention on Artificial Intelligence and Human Rights, Democracy and the Rule of Law (CETS No. 225) is the first international treaty on AI that is binding on the countries that ratify it. The Committee of Ministers of the Council of Europe adopted it on 17 May 2024, and it opened for signature in Vilnius on 5 September 2024. It can be signed by Council of Europe member states, the countries that took part in drafting it, and the European Union. The Kyrgyz Republic is not one of them and has not signed the Convention.

## What it means in practice {#practice}

The Convention does not bind the organization. But it sets a clear expectation: if AI affects a person's life, that person has rights and ways to protect them. The same ideas are making their way into national laws and into the requirements of European partners, so it pays to organize our work in advance so that we can answer these questions.

What this means in everyday work:

- If a decision about a customer is made with the help of AI, it should be clear how it was reached, and the customer should be able to challenge it and have it reviewed by a person.
- People should know when they are dealing with AI rather than with an employee.
- Keep a record of where and how AI is used in a process: without such records you can neither explain a decision nor handle a complaint.
- Use only the tools approved in your organization, and do not put customer data into them without permission.

## Key ideas {#ideas}

- **The whole lifecycle.** The Convention covers everything that happens to an AI system: from design and development to use and retirement.
- **Public and private sectors.** For public authorities and those acting on their behalf, the Convention's rules are mandatory. Each country decides for itself how to meet the obligation in the private sector: by applying the same rules or by taking other measures.
- **Seven principles.** Human dignity and individual autonomy; transparency and oversight; accountability and responsibility; equality and non-discrimination; privacy and personal data protection; reliability; safe innovation, for example testing in a controlled environment.
- **Remedies.** Information about an AI system sufficient for a person to challenge a decision; the right to lodge a complaint; notice that a person is interacting with AI.
- **Risk assessment.** Countries must identify, assess, and reduce the risks AI poses to human rights, democracy, and the rule of law, that is, carry out an [[impact-assessment|impact assessment]] following a [[risk-based-approach|risk-based approach]], and introduce bans where needed. The Council of Europe has developed the HUDERIA methodology for this assessment.
- **Exceptions.** The Convention does not apply to national security, defense, or research on systems that are not yet in use.

## Good to know {#details}

- Adopted on 17 May 2024 and opened for signature on 5 September 2024. On the same day, the European Commission signed it on behalf of the EU.
- It will enter into force on the first day of the month following three months after five signatories, including at least three Council of Europe member states, have ratified it.
- The European Union was the first to ratify it, on 15 May 2026. According to official Council of Europe documents from mid-2026, the Convention had not yet entered into force. We could not confirm its status as of October 2026: the current list of signatures and ratifications is kept by the Council of Europe Treaty Office (link below).
- Each party establishes or designates an independent mechanism to oversee compliance with the Convention; the Conference of the Parties provides overall follow-up.
- In the EU, the Convention is implemented through the EU Artificial Intelligence Act (AI Act).
- The official languages of the text are English and French; we did not find an official Russian translation.
